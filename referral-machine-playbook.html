THE STARTING POINT

Good reputation, no system behind it.

The example running through this guide is a concierge medicine practice selling a genuinely high-trust, high-ticket service — the kind of purchase nobody makes from a cold ad alone. THE SEQUENCE

Six moves, in the order they were actually proposed.

Check items off as you complete them in your own business — your progress is saved automatically on this device.

01
Stop hoping. Start designing.

Turn word of mouth into a system, not an accident

Most businesses treat referrals as something that happens to them, if they're lucky, rather than something they actively build. The fix is to give every happy customer a specific, valuable reason to refer — and to ask at every touchpoint, not just once at the end of a great experience.

The goal isn't a vague "tell your friends." It's a repeatable mechanism that runs the same way, every time, regardless of how memorable any one interaction happened to be.

KEY TERM · REFERRAL MACHINE

A system that makes referrals a built-in part of doing business, not a lucky accident. Instead of hoping happy customers mention you, give them a specific, valuable reason to — and ask at every touchpoint, not just once.

Do this

  • Identify every touchpoint where a happy customer could refer someone
  • Design one specific, valuable reason to refer at each of them
  • Make the ask a standard part of the process, not a special favor
  • Track referrals as a number, not a feeling
Referrals were already converting at a high rate — the missing piece was simply a system to generate more of them on purpose.
02
A coupon feels cheap. A gift feels generous.

Design referrals so the referrer gains status, not just savings

People refer friends more readily when doing so makes them look generous, not merely helpful. A discount code for both parties feels transactional — almost like asking a favor. A real treatment gifted to a friend feels like a genuine gift, with the giver getting the credit.

The shift is small in mechanics but large in psychology: instead of "you both get $500 off," it becomes "bring a friend to your next visit and give them this experience, on us."

KEY TERM · SOCIAL CAPITAL POSITIVE REFERRALS

People refer friends when doing so makes them look good — not when it feels like doing the business a favor. Design the referral so the person making it gains status, not just savings.

The same swap — cash discount to status upgrade — plays out with real estate partners in The Value Leader Playbook.

Do this

  • Replace discount-style referral rewards with a real experience or product
  • Frame the reward as something the referrer gives, not something they get
  • Price it so the perceived value is high but your actual cost is low
  • Test the new framing against your old one and compare referral rates
A guest treatment worth far more than its delivery cost reframed referring from "a favor" into "a gift" — at almost no extra cost.
03
Never ask for a referral again

Build the ask into every appointment, automatically

Asking for referrals directly can feel awkward, both for the business and the customer — which is exactly why it so often doesn't happen consistently. The fix is to remove the awkward ask entirely by making it part of the normal booking flow.

Book the customer's next visit before they leave, then simply ask who they're bringing with them next time. It becomes a standard question, not a favor being requested — and it turns every single appointment into a built-in referral moment.

KEY TERM · BAM-FAM

Book the customer's next visit before they leave, then ask who they're bringing next time. It turns every appointment into a natural referral moment, without anyone ever having to "ask for referrals."

Do this

  • Book every customer's next appointment before they leave the current one
  • Add "who are you bringing next time?" as a standard closing question
  • Train the whole team to ask it the same way, every time
  • Track how many booked guests actually convert into new customers
Turning the ask into a standard question — not a special favor — made it something the whole team could do consistently.
04
Information doesn't convert. Instructions do.

Give every piece of communication an actual job

A newsletter, an email, a social post — none of it does anything for the business unless it asks the reader to take a specific next step. Being "low pressure" is often confused with having no call to action at all, but the two aren't the same thing.

Even a soft, single-line ask — a P.S. mentioning a giveaway, an invitation to enter — gives a channel that was previously just informational an actual chance to generate business.

KEY TERM · CTA (CALL TO ACTION)

The specific action you want someone to take next — book a call, enter a giveaway, refer a friend. Communication without one is just information; nobody acts on information alone.

Do this

  • Audit your last few newsletters or updates for a clear CTA
  • Add one specific, low-pressure ask to your next communication
  • Make the ask easy: entering, referring, replying — not a big commitment
  • Measure response before and after adding it
A single soft-touch P.S. line gave a previously silent newsletter its first real reason to generate a response.
05
Cold ads struggle here. This doesn't.

Replace cold funnels with warm, in-person events

Expensive, high-trust purchases rarely convert well from a cold ad pointed straight at a checkout page — there simply hasn't been time to build the trust a big decision requires. In-person events flip that: your ideal customers are already gathered somewhere, and a live demonstration lets them experience the product before ever being asked to buy.

Rather than competing for cold attention online, the better play is showing up — or hosting — wherever the right audience already spends time, bringing something tangible to demonstrate on the spot.

KEY TERM · EVENT-BASED MARKETING

Rather than advertising to strangers online, you go to — or host — in-person gatherings where your ideal customers already show up, and bring a live demonstration they can experience on the spot.

Do this

  • List local events where your ideal customers already gather
  • Bring a tangible demo people can experience in the moment
  • Schedule a close soon after the demo, while trust is highest
  • Track which events convert well enough to repeat every year
A single strong event can be worth pursuing again every year — the goal is finding the few that reliably work, then repeating them.
06
Say the big number before you need to

Use a giveaway to make the price feel familiar, not shocking

Announcing a high price for the first time, in the middle of a sales conversation, is when it lands hardest. A giveaway lets you say the same number much earlier — and out loud, to everyone — so that by the time someone's actually deciding whether to buy, the number is already familiar instead of alarming.

The offer itself doesn't need to turn a profit. Its job is pulling the right people in; the real return comes from the customers it creates afterward, not the giveaway itself.

KEY TERM · STICKER SHOCK

The jolt of hearing a big number for the first time. Announcing that same number earlier, as the prize in a giveaway, means it's already familiar by the time someone's deciding whether to buy.

Do this

  • Choose your highest-value offer as the giveaway prize
  • Announce its real value publicly and often
  • Let everyone who doesn't win access a paid version of the same thing
  • Don't worry about the cost of the giveaway itself — track what it brings in after
Being willing to give away the highest-value offer, publicly, was described as one of the biggest mental shifts in the whole plan.

Big, expensive decisions aren't won with a bigger ad budget. They're won with more trust, built earlier, in person, before the price ever comes up.

WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

The plan didn't survive contact with the calendar — the strategy did

Not every case study ends with a clean before-and-after number, and this one is worth including honestly for exactly that reason.

The complication

The plan called for attending an outside event roughly once a week. But the practice's market is seasonal — roughly half the local population leaves for several months a year — and there simply weren't enough existing events to attend at that pace.

Plan met reality

The pivot

Rather than abandon event-based marketing, the practice built its own recurring lecture series at venues where its ideal clients already gather — turning "find other people's events" into "become the event."

Owned, not borrowed

The unplanned upside

Appearing publicly while working through this exact problem generated inbound interest from other physicians wanting to copy the model — a distribution channel nobody had planned for.

Visibility as marketing

The lesson

The specific tactic — attend an existing event weekly — was never the actual goal. The goal was warm, in-person, high-trust demand generation. Once the first vehicle didn't fit the market, switching to a second one kept the strategy intact.

Tactic changed. Strategy didn't.

This is the "push vs. pivot" idea in practice — see it as a standalone principle in Founder Field Notes → Decisions & Mindset.

WHERE PEOPLE GET STUCK

Common ways this sequence goes wrong

The ideas are simple. Here's what tends to derail people when they actually try to apply them.

Assuming your market behaves like every other market

Seasonality, local density, and customer habits vary enormously. A weekly cadence that works in one city may be structurally impossible in another.

Following the letter of a plan over its intent

The specific tactic in any plan is a means, not the goal. When a tactic stops fitting reality, the fix is a new tactic — not abandoning the underlying strategy.

Rewarding referrals with a discount instead of a gift

A coupon-style reward can quietly undercut the very motivation — looking generous — that made someone want to refer a friend in the first place.

Treating a newsletter as an update instead of a channel

Regular communication with real customers is a channel with no media cost. Sending it without ever asking for anything wastes that entirely.
