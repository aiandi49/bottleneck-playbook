THE STARTING POINT

One expert, doing everything, everywhere.

The example running through this guide is a real, three-location dental practice. It's a useful case precisely because it's ordinary: one highly skilled owner was personally required for every high-value step of delivery, so the business could only grow as fast as one calendar allowed.

The bottleneck

Every implant case needed five visits. The founder personally did all five — including the ones that didn't require his specific skill.

Single point of failure

The symptom

New patients waited 2–4 weeks just to be diagnosed. By the time they were seen, some had already gone elsewhere.

Lost revenue

The hidden cost

Pricing had barely moved in years, despite an 85%+ close rate — usually a sign that price is set well below what the market will bear.

Underpriced

The goal

Grow from roughly $2M to $15M a year — without the founder simply working more hours than he already was.

Growth without burnout
THE SEQUENCE

The nine moves, in the order they actually need to happen.

Check items off as you complete them in your own business — your progress is saved automatically on this device.

01
Fix capacity first

Find your bottleneck and hand off everything else

Before spending a dollar on marketing or touching your prices, map exactly how your product or service gets delivered, step by step. In almost every capped business, one person — usually the founder — is personally required for steps that don't actually need their specific skill.

The fix isn't working harder. It's separating the two or three steps that truly require expert judgment from everything else, and building a system so someone else can competently do the rest. In the practice used here, only 2 of 5 treatment visits genuinely required the founder — handing off the other 3 doubled capacity without hiring a second expert.

KEY TERM · POINT OF LEVERAGE

The one move that returns the most result for the effort it costs. Worth hunting for, instead of chasing a long list of minor tweaks.

Do this

  • List every step of delivery, in order, from first contact to finished job
  • Mark which steps truly require your top expert's specific skill
  • Everything else gets handed off — even if it feels uncomfortable at first
  • Script a short "handoff" moment so customers trust the new person
  • Reposition your expert as "the architect," not the only capable hands
Result in the case study: capacity roughly doubled — from ~25–30 clients a month to ~50 — with no new experts hired.
02
Only after capacity is real

Turn up spend on what's already working

Adding marketing to a business that can't yet deliver more work doesn't create growth — it just creates a longer wait list. Once you've genuinely freed up capacity, though, the fastest and lowest-risk growth lever is usually more of what's already converting.

Check your local market's ceiling before assuming you're near it. Most businesses stop increasing ad spend far earlier than the market actually requires.

KEY TERM · SUPPLY CONSTRAINED

A state where demand already exceeds what you can deliver. Adding more marketing here doesn't create growth — it just lengthens the line.

Do this

  • Confirm the capacity fix from Move 1 is actually in place
  • Identify the one channel that already converts reliably
  • Research what your local market can realistically absorb
  • Raise spend in stages, watching close rate as you go
A business spending a modest amount a month in a major metro area is often nowhere near its local ceiling.
03
The most overlooked lever

Raise your price — a high close rate is your permission slip

A close rate north of 80% isn't proof your price is right — it's usually proof it's too low. If almost everyone says yes, the market is telling you it would still say yes at a higher number.

The rule of thumb: keep raising price until the combination of close rate and price actually starts to fall. Selling slightly fewer people at a meaningfully higher price is still a win — you're only in trouble once the total math goes down, not just the close rate.

KEY TERM · PRICE × CLOSE RATE

The number that actually matters. Selling 10% fewer people at 20% more revenue per sale is a net win, not a loss — track the product, not just the percentage.

Do this

  • Calculate your current close rate
  • If it's 80%+, test a 15–20% price increase
  • Track price × close rate together, not close rate alone
  • Keep raising in stages until that combined number actually drops
In the case study, a 20% price increase was followed by close rate going up, not down — average order value nearly doubled over 90 days.
04
Fund growth without new capital

Collect payment upfront, not as-you-go

If customers are going to pay for something eventually anyway, collecting that money sooner — in full or through financing — is one of the simplest cash-flow unlocks available. It shortens the gap between doing the work and having cash to reinvest.

Frame it as a normal choice, not a big ask: "credit card in full, or financing" as the two default options, with pay-as-you-go quietly becoming the fallback rather than the default.

KEY TERM · CASH CONVERSION CYCLE

The gap between making a sale and having that money actually sitting in your account. Getting paid upfront closes most of that gap immediately.

Do this

  • Offer two clear options: pay in full, or finance the full amount
  • Make pay-as-you-go the fallback, not the headline option
  • Collect a payment method at intake, framed as completing a profile
  • Track how much faster cash lands in the bank each month
This single change was reported as the biggest unlock of the entire 90 days — monthly revenue nearly tripled soon after.
05
Why this compounds

Let the virtuous cycle of pricing do the rest

Once price goes up and cash arrives sooner, something else opens up: room to reinvest in the actual experience. Better experience attracts a customer who cares less about price and more about outcome — which supports charging more, which funds even better delivery and talent.

This is the opposite of competing on price. For high-stakes purchases especially, people often read a higher price as lower risk, not higher cost — nobody wants the cheapest option for something irreversible.

KEY TERM · PRICE-QUALITY CORRELATION

For big, irreversible decisions, people often assume a higher price means lower risk. Being the cheapest option can actually make buyers more nervous, not less.

Do this

  • Reinvest new margin into the customer experience, not just profit
  • Resist the urge to compete down on price when a competitor undercuts you
  • Use the extra margin to bring on better talent
  • Let the improved experience justify the next price increase
Higher prices were followed by happier customers — because the extra revenue was visibly reinvested in their experience.
06
Reduce friction, not effort

Tighten the sales process with education, not pressure

Every sales process has two real levers: qualification (getting the right people to show up) and education (getting them into the right mindset before you ask them to buy). If your sales conversations run long or get stuck on the same objections, the fix is usually more education earlier — not a harder close.

A short pre-education video, watched while a customer is already waiting, can cover the pains they recognize, the proof that it works, and the objections they were already going to raise — before the actual conversation even starts.

KEY TERM · VIDEO SALES LETTER (VSL)

A short pre-recorded video that handles part of the sales conversation before it happens — surfacing the problem, showing evidence it can be solved, and getting ahead of objections.

Do this

  • Map every step from first contact to money collected
  • Find the point where customers hesitate or ask the same questions
  • Build a short video: pain, proof, common objections answered
  • Play it during any natural waiting period before the sales conversation
Adding a short video before the pricing conversation was described as pure upside — smoother process, fewer questions, no downside noticed.
07
The most common mistake in the room

Nail it before you scale it

The instinct, especially in brick-and-mortar businesses, is to open another location the moment growth stalls. Almost always, the better move is to make the location you already have significantly more profitable first — because a second unit just multiplies whatever problems the first one still has.

Only once a single location is running near its real ceiling does adding a second one start to make the math meaningfully better, rather than just meaningfully more complicated.

MYTH VS. REALITY

Myth: "we need more locations to make more money." Reality: fix and max out the one you have — that's usually what unlocks the money you were trying to get from a second location anyway.

Do this

  • Confirm the core bottleneck from Move 1 is genuinely solved
  • Push your current location's profitability as high as it will go
  • Only then model the real math on a second location
The practice hit its growth goal without opening a single new location — by making the existing one significantly better instead.
08
The lever that touches everything else

Win on speed to lead

For anyone shopping around, the business that responds first usually wins the customer — often within about 60 seconds, not hours or days. Speed compounds across the entire business: it lowers cost per customer, raises close rates, raises show-up rates, and reduces how much competitors even factor into the decision.

If your process has customers waiting weeks to even be seen, that delay is quietly costing far more revenue than most owners realize — especially for anyone in genuine discomfort or urgency.

KEY TERM · SPEED TO LEAD

The gap between a lead arriving and your first response. People actively comparing options tend to go with whoever calls back first — which is why this is measured in seconds, not business days.

Do this

  • Measure your current average time-to-first-response
  • Remove any queue between a lead arriving and first contact
  • Shrink diagnostic or intake time so people are seen sooner
  • Track speed as a KPI on the same dashboard as revenue
Cutting the booking wait from 2–4 weeks to about 7 days lifted close rates and show-up rates at the same time.
09
Free, and already happening

Let your own results become your marketing

Every business that produces a visible transformation is sitting on marketing it isn't using. The fix is mostly about capture, not creativity: document the before, capture the reveal moment, and post it in more than one format.

Note the sequencing here — organic content is worth doing, but only after the earlier moves. Posting more content into a business that's still capacity-constrained just adds to a backlog instead of adding revenue.

KEY TERM · ORGANIC CONTENT

Anything you publish without paying to promote it. Its reach comes from how much you post and how good it is — not from a media budget.

Do this

  • Capture a documented "before" for every customer, every time
  • Capture the reveal or "after" moment on camera
  • Post it three ways: short story, carousel, and a case-study breakdown
  • End every post with one clear call to action
  • Only prioritize this once Moves 1–8 are already in motion
This lever was intentionally saved for last — it multiplies results once supply and price are already fixed, not before.

Scaling isn't the thing that makes a business profitable. Getting one location, one offer, one process to work exceptionally well is what generates the cash — and the confidence — to expand at all.

WHERE PEOPLE GET STUCK

Common ways this sequence goes wrong

Watching the moves is easy. Here's what tends to derail people when they actually try to apply them.

Scaling before fixing the bottleneck

Adding a location, a channel, or headcount before Move 1 is solved just multiplies the same constraint — it rarely fixes it.

Mistaking a high close rate for the right price

A close rate above 80% usually means there's pricing power sitting unused, not proof the price is already correct.

Treating the founder as irreplaceable

The belief that "no one can do this like I can" is often just an untested assumption — one that quietly caps growth on its own.

Adding marketing to a capacity problem

More leads into a business that can't deliver more work doesn't create growth. It just creates a longer, more frustrated waiting list.
