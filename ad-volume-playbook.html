THE STARTING POINT

A working machine, running on the smallest possible engine.

The example running through this guide is an online education business with a genuinely proven funnel: a small paid entry event that reliably converts a fifth of attendees into paying members. Doubling the number of customers wouldn't strain delivery at all — this was a pure demand problem. And yet growth had quietly stalled, not because the model was broken, but because almost every lever available to push more people into it was left untouched.

THE SEQUENCE

Seven moves, in the order that actually unlocks more spend.

Check items off as you complete them in your own business — your progress is saved automatically on this device.

01
Name the limit before defending it

Stop capping your spend out of instinct

A spending cap that isn't based on an actual number — a real cost-per-acquisition ceiling, a real cash constraint — is usually just a feeling wearing a budget's clothes. If a channel is already profitable at the volume you're running, the question isn't whether to spend more; it's why you haven't already.

The fix starts with distinguishing a real financial limit from a habit of caution that never got questioned.

KEY TERM · CPA (COST PER ACQUISITION)

What it costs, on average, to acquire one customer. A CPA cap should come from real math — what a customer is worth — not from a round number that feels safe.

Do this

  • Calculate the real CPA where a customer stops being profitable
  • Compare that number to your current, self-imposed cap
  • Write down why the current cap exists — if there's no real reason, remove it
  • Commit to one launch where you deliberately test past your old comfort level
There was no actual reason for the existing spend cap — just a pattern of caution that had never been questioned out loud.
02
Spend on purpose, not evenly

Follow a spend cadence instead of a flat budget

Spreading ad spend evenly across a launch wastes the early days, when you don't yet know which creative works, and undersells the final days, when urgency is highest and performance is usually strongest. A deliberate cadence — small in week one, heavier in the middle, heaviest right before the close — spends money when it actually works hardest.

Roughly: a small share early purely to test creative, a steady middle stretch once you know what's working, and as much as half the total budget concentrated in the final few days.

KEY CONCEPT · SPEND CADENCE

Spending a small share early to test creative, then scaling steadily, then concentrating the largest share of the budget in the final days of a launch — instead of spreading it evenly throughout.

Do this

  • Allocate roughly 5-10% of total spend to first-week creative testing
  • Scale steadily through the middle of the launch on what's already working
  • Plan to spend as much as half your total budget in the final few days
  • Track performance by phase, not just by launch total
A clear, repeatable cadence turns "how much should I spend today" into a decision that's already been made in advance.
03
Your customers are already making your ads

Turn your community into your creative engine

Going from a handful of ad creatives to hundreds doesn't require a bigger production budget — it requires capturing content that's already being created. Screenshots from an active community, clips from live sessions, and customers' own posts are all usable ad material sitting unused.

Adding a small, specific incentive — unlocking a bonus for posting a video of themselves using the product in the first week — turns onboarding into an ad-generation engine at the same time, at almost no added cost.

KEY CONCEPT · THE CUSTOMER CREATIVE LOOP

Incentivizing new customers to create and post their own content early on turns each new customer into a source of future ad creative — compounding as your customer base grows.

Do this

  • Pull existing screenshots, clips, and posts from your community as ad material
  • Create a specific, small unlock for new customers who post themselves using the product
  • Make this part of your standard onboarding, not a one-off request
  • Set a target creative count in the hundreds, not the single digits
A single incentivized activation step was projected to turn every cohort of new customers into another batch of ready-made ads.
04
Talk to the pain, not just the product

Widen your message to unlock real scale

Every audience splits into people who already know your product, people who know they have the problem you solve, and people who don't yet realize either. Advertising that only speaks to the first group will always plateau quickly, because that group is small and finite.

Reaching people earlier in that spectrum — leading with the pain or problem itself, rather than the specific offer — is what allows spend to keep scaling without performance collapsing.

KEY CONCEPT · LEVELS OF AWARENESS

A framework describing how audiences range from already familiar with your exact offer, down to not yet aware they have the problem at all. Scaling spend requires messaging that reaches further down that range, not just the warmest end of it.

Do this

  • Audit your current hooks: are they mostly about your product, or about the pain it solves?
  • Write new hooks that name the problem before mentioning the solution
  • Test pain-led hooks against product-led hooks at increasing spend levels
  • Expect some rise in cost as you scale — watch for it tripling, not just rising
Messaging built only around the product was identified as the ceiling on how far spend could realistically scale.
05
Fewer choices, better outcome

Make the default path the only path

Offering both a cheaper and a better option side by side tends to pull more people toward the cheaper one, simply because it's there. Presenting only the option you actually want people to take — with a compelling reason attached — removes that pull entirely.

Pairing a single, higher-value option with a physical bonus reserved specifically for it gives people a concrete reason to commit to the better path, rather than defaulting to the cheaper one out of habit.

KEY CONCEPT · SINGLE-PATH OFFER

Presenting only the option you want someone to take, instead of a menu that includes a cheaper alternative. Removing the comparison removes the pull toward the lesser option.

Do this

  • Identify your higher-value pricing option
  • Make it the only option presented during your next promotion
  • Add a tangible bonus available only through that option
  • Compare take-rate against your previous multi-option offer
Presenting only the higher-value option, with nothing cheaper alongside it, tripled the rate at which people chose it.
06
Test the bold idea safely

Isolate a big structural change with a clean split test

A genuinely different approach — restructuring an entire funnel, not just tweaking a price — is worth testing, but not worth betting everything on blind. Running it as a true split test, at the exact same price point as the current approach, isolates the one variable that actually matters: does the new structure convert better?

This turns a risky-sounding idea into a contained experiment, with a clear way to know whether it actually outperforms what's already working before committing to it fully.

KEY CONCEPT · A RISK-ISOLATED SPLIT TEST

Testing a bold new structure against your current one at an identical price point, so any difference in results comes from the structure itself — not from an unrelated change like price.

Do this

  • Design your bold new structure at the same price as your current offer
  • Split traffic evenly between the two versions
  • Keep everything else in the funnel identical between both groups
  • Let the results decide — resist declaring a winner early
Structuring the boldest idea as a clean, equal-price test made it feel like a manageable experiment instead of a risky bet.
07
Go deep, not wide

Narrow your targeting to actually scale spend

The instinct when trying to spend more is often to broaden targeting to reach more people at once. In practice, the opposite tends to work better: many narrow, specific audience segments, each with their own message, consistently outperform one broad message aimed at everyone.

Scaling spend well is less about finding one enormous audience and more about draining many smaller, well-defined ones — and adding new ones as each existing pocket saturates.

KEY CONCEPT · MANY PONDS, NOT ONE OCEAN

Targeting many small, specific audience segments with tailored messaging outperforms targeting one enormous, generic audience with a single message — even when the total spend is the same.

Do this

  • Break your broad audience into several specific, named segments
  • Write distinct messaging for each segment rather than one generic version
  • Track performance separately by segment, not just in aggregate
  • Add new segments as existing ones start to saturate
Treating the audience as many specific groups, rather than one broad market, was identified as the actual key to scaling spend further.

WHERE PEOPLE GET STUCK

Common ways this sequence goes wrong

The ideas are simple to agree with. Here's what tends to derail people when they try to apply them.

Capping spend on a feeling instead of a number

A spending limit that isn't based on real CPA math is just caution wearing a budget's clothes — and it quietly caps growth on a channel that's already working.

Running one launch's worth of creative forever

A handful of ads can't support a meaningfully larger budget. Volume has to scale alongside spend, not lag behind it.

Broadening your message when you should narrow it

Trying to reach everyone with one message usually performs worse than reaching many specific groups with several.

Testing a bold idea by betting everything on it

A genuinely new structure deserves a fair, isolated test — not a full switch before you know whether it actually outperforms what you have.
