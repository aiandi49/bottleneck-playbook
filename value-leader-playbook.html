THE STARTING POINT

Already good. Still interchangeable.

On the phone, the business still sounded exactly like every competitor: an hourly rate, and not much else.

THE SEQUENCE

Seven moves, fixed back to front: offer, then sales, then traffic.

Check items off as you complete them in your own business — your progress is saved automatically on this device.

01
Name the trap before fixing it

Stop competing as a commodity

When a customer looks at two options and genuinely can't tell them apart, they will take the cheaper one every time — not because price is what they care about most, but because it's the only thing left to compare. Being measured in the exact same unit as every competitor — an hourly rate, a flat fee — guarantees that comparison happens.

The fix isn't necessarily changing the pricing model. It's making sure a customer has more than one number to think about before they decide.

KEY CONCEPT · THE COMMODITY PROBLEM

If a customer can look at two options and conclude they're the same, they will always choose the cheaper one. The way out isn't necessarily being cheapest — it's giving them real reasons the two aren't actually the same thing.

Do this

  • List what a customer currently compares you on (usually just price)
  • List everything real that makes your service different
  • Check how many of those differences are ever mentioned out loud
  • Decide whether you're playing the cheapest game or the best game — pick one on purpose
The business already had real differentiators — better materials, no hidden fees — that simply weren't part of the sales conversation yet.
02
Give customers something else to compare

Build a tiered offer around what competitors never mention

Three things quietly drive most purchase decisions: how fast something happens, how safe or risky it feels, and how easy it is. A business that never talks about any of the three is leaving the entire decision to price by default.

Building a clear "premium" tier — vetted staff, full materials included, a guaranteed cap on cost — gives a customer with money to spend an obvious way to spend more, while a simpler tier still exists for anyone who genuinely just wants the cheapest option.

KEY CONCEPT · SPEED, RISK, EASE

Three levers that differentiate a service beyond price: how fast you deliver it, how safe the customer feels using it, and how easy it is for them. Most businesses only compete on price because they've never priced these three separately.

Do this

  • Create a premium tier that bundles your best people, materials, and guarantees
  • Keep a standard tier for genuinely price-sensitive customers
  • Always present the premium tier first
  • Let people who want to spend less say so — don't assume it
A internally-certified "top tier" staff designation cost almost nothing to create, but gave the premium option a concrete reason to exist.
03
Remove the fear nobody says out loud

Add a cap that kills the biggest hidden objection

For any service priced by time or usage, the unspoken fear is always the same: what if this costs far more than expected? Most competitors never address it, which means simply naming it — and guaranteeing a ceiling on cost — can be a meaningful edge on its own.

The guarantee doesn't have to be generous to the point of risk. It just has to exist, clearly, before the customer has to ask.

KEY CONCEPT · SEEDS OF DOUBT

Naming a real risk your competitors don't address — and showing you've already solved it — plants a doubt about the cheaper option without ever criticizing it directly.

Do this

  • Identify the biggest unspoken fear about your type of service
  • Build a guarantee or cap that directly answers it
  • State it clearly and early, before a customer has to ask
  • Ask competitors' customers (informally) whether they were ever offered the same
A simple price cap, rarely offered by competitors, reframed the entire pricing conversation around safety instead of just the hourly number.
04
A cash reward isn't always the best reward

Turn referral partners into status-gainers, not bounty hunters

A flat referral discount treats a partner like a commission-based lead source. But for someone whose reputation depends on being trusted, being able to say "I got you upgraded to the premium experience, for free" often means far more than a small kickback — because it makes them look good to the person they referred.

Swapping a cash-style discount for a premium upgrade costs a similar amount to deliver, but changes what the referral actually represents to the person making it.

KEY CONCEPT · A NEW OFFER TO PARTNERS

Instead of a small discount, give the referred customer a free upgrade to your premium tier. The partner looks generous and well-connected; the customer gets a noticeably better experience; the cost to you barely changes.

Do this

  • Replace a flat referral discount with a free upgrade to your best tier
  • Frame it to partners as something that makes them look good
  • Ask referred customers to mention where they heard about you
  • Compare referral volume before and after the change
A single referral channel was already generating roughly triple the profit of an average job — before this change was even made.
05
One relationship can replace twenty

Go after the organization, not just the individual

Winning over one well-connected individual is good. Winning over the organization they belong to — where dozens of similar people work — multiplies that same relationship many times over, for roughly the same amount of relationship-building effort.

Once one contact inside an organization responds well, showing up in person to meet the wider group and offering the same premium treatment to everyone there turns a single relationship into an entire referral network at once.

KEY CONCEPT · THE ORGANIZATION-LEVEL DEAL

Rather than winning one partner at a time, offer better terms to an entire group at once, in exchange for being the default choice their whole network refers to.

Do this

  • Identify organizations made up of many potential individual partners
  • Land one strong relationship inside the organization first
  • Show up in person to meet the wider group once you have a foothold
  • Offer the group a deal, in exchange for being the default recommendation
A single organization-level relationship was identified as the single highest-leverage move available — worth far more than another round of individual outreach.
06
Generic outreach reads as generic

Personalize outreach at scale instead of blasting everyone the same message

A single templated message sent to hundreds of contacts performs worse than the same idea, lightly personalized to each recipient — and it's also more likely to get flagged as spam. What used to require real time per message can now be done automatically, at volume, using each contact's own available details.

The goal isn't to fake a relationship. It's to make sure the very first message actually acknowledges who it's going to, instead of reading like it went to everyone.

KEY CONCEPT · PERSONALIZATION AT SCALE

Using each contact's own details to customize outreach automatically, rather than sending one identical message to an entire list. It performs better and is less likely to be filtered out entirely.

Do this

  • Build a list with whatever personal details are realistically available
  • Generate a personalized opening line for each contact automatically
  • Quickly review the batch before sending, rather than sending blind
  • Test personalized outreach against a generic version to confirm the lift
Personalizing the very first message was expected to noticeably lift response rates while also reducing the odds of being marked as spam.
07
The proof was already happening on-site

Make your own team part of the content engine

Asking for a review as the owner can feel transactional. Having the person actually on-site ask, on behalf of a small reward for the team rather than the business, tends to get a noticeably better response — because it's framed as helping a person, not a company.

Pairing that with a simple before-and-after clip from every job — empty to full, then full to empty — turns routine work into proof, without requiring any extra production effort.

KEY TERM · BEFORE/AFTER SOCIAL POST

A content format where you show a transformation — in this case, a space before and after a job — as simple, repeatable proof of what you actually deliver.

Do this

  • Have your on-site team lead ask for reviews, not the owner
  • Offer a small team-level reward tied to review volume
  • Capture a simple before-and-after clip on every job
  • Close each post with the review it earned
Making the ask about the team's reward, not the owner's request, was expected to meaningfully raise how often customers actually left a review.

It's often easier to sell one high-value offer to one customer with real money than to sell many small, cheap versions of it to a crowd that's counting every dollar.

THE DECISION

Rejected — but not because the plan was wrong

Not every case study in this hub ends with a golden ticket, and this one is worth including honestly for exactly that reason.

The verdict

After building out this entire sequence, the answer was still a "fail" for the purposes of the show's year-long mentorship prize.

Not selected

The actual reason

The plan itself was never in question — it was described as something that would clearly work. The concern was one of stage: the business was judged to be further from multi-location growth than the format was built to support.

A fit decision, not a quality one

The reaction

Rather than treat the decision as a verdict on the business, the founder committed to running the full 90-day plan anyway, prize or not.

Executed regardless

The lesson

A "no" tied to timing or fit is a different thing entirely from a "no" tied to merit. Worth separating the two clearly before internalizing either one as the final word.

Rejection ≠ failure

This pairs directly with Move 07 of the Bottleneck Playbook — nailing one location before chasing more of them.

WHERE PEOPLE GET STUCK

Common ways this sequence goes wrong

The ideas are simple to agree with. Here's what tends to derail people when they try to apply them.

Trying to win a price war you didn't choose

If your service can be compared on a single shared number, it will be — the fix is adding real, named differences, not just adjusting that number.

Treating a strong referral channel as a side experiment

A channel quietly outperforming everything else by several times deserves direct investment, not an occasional mention.

Sending one message to an entire list

Generic outreach at scale often converts worse, and risks looking like spam, compared to the same message lightly personalized.

Treating a "no" about timing as a "no" about quality

Not every rejection is a verdict on the work. Sometimes it's simply about fit, stage, or format — worth checking before drawing the larger conclusion.
