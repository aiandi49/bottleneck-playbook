THE STARTING POINT

A great close rate, aimed at the wrong jobs.

The example running through this guide is a young roofing company that was already doing something most new businesses can't: reliably converting doors into deals. The problem wasn't demand or skill — it was that almost all of the attention was going toward big, competitive replacement jobs, while a much bigger, quieter opportunity sat in the smaller repair work nobody else wanted to chase.

The overlooked stream

Roughly 70% of revenue came from full replacements, but the smaller repair jobs — only 30% of revenue — faced almost no competition, since larger companies didn't want them.

Repairs, undervalued

The bottleneck

One founder was still doing nearly everything personally — sales, delivery, and follow-up — with only a few weeks of true operating time freed up all year.

Time & capacity gaps

The asset already in hand

Door-to-door sales were already converting at a rate most businesses would envy — the channel wasn't broken, it just wasn't being pushed hard enough.

An underused strength

The goal

Grow from roughly $20K a month to a $7–10 million a year business within three years — without losing the reliability that was already working.

Scale without losing what works
THE SEQUENCE

Seven moves, in the order they were actually made.

Check items off as you complete them in your own business — your progress is saved automatically on this device.

01
Look where competitors won't

Find the profit sitting in the jobs everyone else skips

Every industry has a version of "the job nobody wants." It's usually smaller, less glamorous, and skipped by bigger competitors who only chase the big-ticket work. That's exactly what makes it valuable: less competition, faster sales cycles, and a customer who remembers you were the one company willing to show up for something small.

In this case, that meant treating repair work — not full replacements — as a real product line worth systematizing, instead of a distraction from the "real" business.

KEY TERM · GROSS PROFIT

Revenue minus the direct cost of delivering the job — materials and labor. It's the number that actually tells you which revenue stream is worth doubling down on.

Do this

  • List every job type you currently do, big and small
  • Calculate gross profit per job type, not just revenue
  • Identify which smaller job type has the least competition
  • Ask whether you could realistically sell 3x as many of it
Repair jobs faced almost no competition — larger companies simply didn't want them, leaving the volume wide open.
02
Volume beats a bigger single ticket

Raise the average ticket on your overlooked revenue stream

Once a smaller, less-contested job type is chosen, the next move is making each one worth more — without turning it into the big-ticket item it was deliberately chosen to avoid competing with. Small increases in what's included per visit compound fast across high volume.

Here, that meant working to lift the average repair ticket several times over, simply by bundling more of the related small jobs into each visit rather than treating them as one-off callouts.

KEY TERM · AVERAGE TICKET

The average dollar amount of a single sale. Raising it — even modestly — multiplies across every job you already have the capacity to do.

Do this

  • Calculate your current average ticket for the target job type
  • List every related small job a customer might also need
  • Set a target ticket that's meaningfully higher, not just marginally
  • Train the team to look for the bundle, not just the callout
The target was raising the average repair ticket several times over — turning a $1,500 callout into a $2,500–4,000 visit.
03
Sell before anyone picks up the phone

Warm up every prospect before a human ever talks to them

By the time a salesperson gets on a call or knocks on a door, most of the customer's hesitation is still sitting there unaddressed. A short video watched beforehand can carry that weight instead — building trust, showing proof, and pre-answering the objections that would otherwise eat up the live conversation.

Using two different videos for two different moments works even better: one that builds credibility before a rep ever shows up, and a second, more specific one right before the closing conversation that primes the customer for the additional work they didn't know they needed.

KEY TERM · VSL (VIDEO SALES LETTER)

A pre-recorded video that explains and sells your product before a prospect ever talks to a salesperson, so they arrive at the conversation already warmed up.

Do this

  • Script a short video that builds trust before the first visit
  • Include real proof: past customers, results, credentials
  • Pre-answer the 3-4 objections that come up most often
  • Build a second, more specific video for right before the close
Two short videos — one on-site, one pre-close — did real selling work before a closer ever picked up the phone.
04
Don't get distracted by shiny new channels

Go all-in on the channel that's already working

It's tempting to chase a new acquisition channel the moment growth becomes a goal. But a channel that's already converting well deserves more resourcing before anything new gets added — diversifying too early usually just splits attention away from what's proven.

With one channel already converting at an exceptional rate, the clear move was recruiting and structuring an entire team around it, rather than spreading effort across several unproven ones.

KEY TERM · ACQUISITION CHANNEL

Any repeatable way you get customers — door-knocking, referrals, content, or paid ads. The goal isn't more channels; it's more of the one that already works.

Do this

  • Identify your single best-converting acquisition channel
  • Turn its commission or incentive math into something simple to explain
  • Recruit specifically for that channel before adding a new one
  • Resist adding a second channel until the first is fully resourced
Going all-in on the one channel that already worked led to more than four times the prior sales velocity.
05
The unlock that mattered most

Sell more, honestly, with a menu instead of a pitch

A menu-style close works because it starts by giving something up. Telling a customer what they don't need first feels unusual enough that it builds real trust — before ever prescribing what they do need. From there, the choice isn't "yes or no," it's a simple either/or that makes saying yes the path of least resistance.

This close was described as the single highest-leverage change made in the whole process — not because it was aggressive, but because it made honesty itself part of the sales pitch.

KEY TERM · THE MENU CLOSE

Present options like a menu: first tell the customer what they don't need — this builds trust — then prescribe what they do need, and close with a simple either/or choice on the payment method already on file.

Do this

  • Start every close by naming what the customer doesn't need
  • Prescribe specifically what they do need, with a clear reason why
  • Offer a simple either/or choice instead of a plain yes/no
  • Close on a payment method already on file to remove friction
The consultative, "here's what you don't need" approach was credited as the single most effective change made to the sales process.
06
One job shouldn't be the end of the relationship

Turn one-time jobs into recurring revenue

A small annual membership rarely pays for itself in cash collected upfront — and that isn't really the point. The real value is a guaranteed, welcomed reason to be back on the property every year, with a warm relationship and a natural opening to sell more.

Crediting the membership fee toward future repairs, and discounting it further in exchange for a review, turns a minor add-on into a dependable stream of future appointments.

KEY TERM · LTV (LIFETIME VALUE)

The gross profit collected over the entire lifespan of a customer — in other words, how much a customer is really worth once every future job is counted, not just the first one.

Do this

  • Design a low-cost annual membership tied to your core service
  • Credit the membership fee toward future work, so it's never wasted spend
  • Offer a discount in exchange for a review to build both loyalty and proof
  • Treat the annual visit as a sales appointment, not just a checkup
The real value of the membership was never the small fee — it was the guaranteed annual appointment to sell more.
07
Pure margin, no extra cost

Let customers pay to skip the line

When demand already exceeds capacity, the order jobs get done in is a decision, not a fixed schedule. Charging a premium to move to the front of the line costs nothing extra to deliver — the same total work gets done either way — which makes it close to pure margin.

This works especially well anywhere speed already matters to the customer emotionally, not just practically — a leaking roof, a broken system, anything that feels urgent even when it technically isn't an emergency.

KEY TERM · SUPPLY CONSTRAINED

Having more demand than you can currently deliver. In this state, a speed premium is one of the simplest ways to add margin — the same idea shows up in the Bottleneck Playbook's Move 02.

Do this

  • Confirm you're genuinely supply constrained, not just busy
  • Set a clear priority fee for moving to the front of the queue
  • Offer it at the natural moment: right after the main sale closes
  • Track it as pure margin, since delivery cost doesn't change
A simple priority fee turned a same-week job into extra margin, with the customer thrilled to skip the wait.

The businesses that win in high-volume, low-glamour work aren't competing with anyone — because everyone else decided the job wasn't worth doing well.

WHERE PEOPLE GET STUCK

Common ways this sequence goes wrong

The moves are simple to describe. Here's what tends to derail people when they actually try to apply them.

Chasing the glamorous job over the profitable one

Bigger tickets feel more like "real" business, but a smaller job with less competition and higher volume can quietly out-earn it.

Adding channels before maxing out the one that works

A new acquisition channel splits attention away from the one already converting well, right when it deserves more resourcing, not less.

Turning the menu close into a script instead of a judgment call

The "what you don't need" step only builds trust if it's genuinely true. Recommending everything defeats the entire mechanism.

Undervaluing what a small membership actually buys

A recurring fee that barely covers its own cost can still be extremely valuable — because what it really buys is a yearly appointment to sell more.
