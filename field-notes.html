Founder Field Notes

The other guides in this hub follow one business, in order, from problem to fix. This one doesn't. It's a running reference of ideas worth keeping in your back pocket — pulled from a wide-ranging, unusually candid conversation about starting, pricing, hiring, growth, and the thinking behind all of it. Dip in wherever it's useful.

01

Starting

The four things that actually make you a business owner

Not a business plan, not a logo, not permission. Just: an entity, a bank account, a way to accept payment, and asking a stranger — once — for money in exchange for something. Do those four things and the title has already changed from "aspiring" to "owner."

Fear lives in the vague, never in the specific

"I might fail" is unanswerable, which is exactly why it feels so heavy. "What happens if I ask 100 people and all 100 say no" has an actual answer: you adjust the offer and ask 100 more. Naming the specific version of the fear usually shrinks it.

An unmade decision can last forever

A choice you commit to gets corrected by feedback — you learn something and move again. A choice you never make just sits there, indefinitely, teaching you nothing. Most people lose more time standing at the fork than they would have lost picking the wrong path.

The earliest stage is the easiest to navigate

It doesn't feel that way, but it's true: with nothing built yet, the next step is the same for everyone. As a business grows, the number of reasonable next steps multiplies — so "not knowing what to do" is far more a beginning-stage feeling than people expect, and it never fully goes away.

02

Pricing & Offers

Don't sell out of your own wallet

If something is easy for you, it's tempting to price it like it's easy for everyone. What actually matters is how much the customer doesn't want to do it themselves — not how simple it felt on your end.

A four-question way to find your real price

Ask real prospects: at what price is this too expensive to consider? Too cheap to trust? Getting expensive, but you'd still buy? A genuine bargain? Plotting the answers reveals a real range — instead of guessing, or charging what you personally feel comfortable asking for.

Price is a forcing function, not a guess

In a service business especially, price tends to track how far along the owner actually is. When demand keeps outpacing what you can deliver, that's the signal to raise price — not a reason to feel guilty about it.

Add a zero, then work backward

Take whatever price feels reasonable and multiply it by ten. Then ask what would genuinely have to be true — what service, speed, or access — to make that number worth it. The answer usually reveals a premium version of the offer worth testing on a handful of people before it ever needs to scale.

03

Hiring & Team

There are no unicorns

Founders often go looking for one person who can do everything they currently do. That person doesn't exist, and the search for them rarely ends. What usually exists instead is two or three people who are each excellent at one piece of it.

Teach it in a tenth of the time it took you

You learned your job the slow way — through trial and error, and plenty of dead ends. When training someone else, teach it the way you wish someone had taught you: straight to what actually works, skipping the mistakes you no longer need them to repeat.

Whoever's most responsible sets the bar

In any given area, the person who owns the outcome should hold the highest standard for what "good enough" means there. If someone else's bar is genuinely higher than yours, that's usually a sign they should be the one running it.

Price your referral bonus like it's actually worth something

A business paying a flat, small bonus for a referral that generates six figures in gross profit isn't protecting its margins — it's underpricing its own best growth channel. If a referral is worth $250,000, a bonus in the tens of thousands is still a great trade.

04

Growth & Retention

Two businesses, same revenue, very different futures

Picture two companies that each reach $3M in year three. One got there by replacing a shrinking pool of customers every single year. The other kept its customers and simply stacked new ones on top. Both show the same number on paper — only one of them gets easier to grow from here.

Almost every growth problem is one of two problems

Either not enough people know you exist yet, or you already have more interest than you can currently handle. The second one is a good problem to have — but it's still a real constraint, and it calls for a different fix than more marketing.

The hiring pipeline and the sales pipeline are the same pipeline

Generating interest, following up, making the case, onboarding, and keeping someone engaged over time — that sequence works whether the "someone" is a customer or a new hire. A business that's good at one and stuck on the other can usually just reuse its own playbook.

Automating a process isn't the same as fixing a constraint

It's possible to spend heavily automating a task that was already working fine — and still not touch the actual thing limiting growth. Before automating anything, it's worth checking whether it was ever the bottleneck in the first place.

05

Decisions & Mindset

Long-term thinking changes the foundation, not just the timeline

Something meant to last five years and something meant to last fifty need genuinely different foundations from day one — not just a different amount of patience applied to the same plan.

A bad day isn't a strategy meeting

Feeling discouraged today usually isn't enough evidence to justify changing a plan you built with a clear head. If the dissatisfaction is about your whole situation, it's worth acting on. If it's really just about today, it usually isn't.

Push if the thesis holds. Pivot if it doesn't.

If a core assumption the business was built on turns out to be false, that's a real reason to change direction. If everything you believed at the start still looks true and it's simply taking longer than hoped, that's usually a reason to keep going instead.

See this play out in a real seasonal-market pivot in The Referral Machine Playbook.

Outsourcing your hardest thinking makes you weaker at it

Tools that offer instant answers are genuinely useful for the easy decisions. But judgment is a skill, and skills that go unused tend to fade — so the highest-stakes calls are worth continuing to make yourself, deliberately, even when a faster answer is one click away.
